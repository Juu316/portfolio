The most valuable thing you can put in a portfolio is evidence of how you think. A post that walks through one real decision does more than a list of frameworks ever will. Here is a structure that works.

The problem

Open with the concrete situation. What was broken, slow, or missing? Give a number if you have one — "the dashboard took 6 seconds to load" beats "the dashboard was slow", because the number proves you measured.

What I tried first

Say what did not work. This is the part almost everyone skips, and it is the part that reads as genuine experience. An engineer who only ever describes their successful approach sounds like they are describing someone else's.

What actually worked

Explain the fix and, more importantly, why it worked:

  • What was the actual root cause?
  • What made this solution the right shape for it?
  • What did you deliberately choose not to do, and why?

Show the code if a snippet makes it land:

// A short, real snippet beats a long, invented one.
// Ten lines is usually plenty.

What it cost

Every decision has a downside. Naming yours is the strongest possible signal that you understand the tradeoff rather than having copied it from a blog post. Was it more complexity? Extra memory? A dependency you now maintain?

What I would do differently

Close with the honest retrospective. This is what an interviewer will actually ask you about, so having thought it through in writing means you will answer it well out loud.

Writing tips for this section of your portfolio:

  • Three good posts beat ten thin ones. Do not pad the list.
  • Write about work you can discuss in depth — you will be asked.
  • Date them honestly. A gap is fine; a fake date is not.
  • Delete this template once you have written a real post.
